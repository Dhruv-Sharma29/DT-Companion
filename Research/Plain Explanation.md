# 📄 Plain Explanation

**9 Sep 2026.** The project explained straightforwardly — no persuasion, no positioning.
For the argued and defended version see [[MASTER — Full Problem Brief]].

---

## In two lines #key

> AI gives every aspiring founder the same average answer. So student startup aspirants commit
> to the first problem that sounds right — often one already crowded — instead of the
> differentiated one their own evidence actually supports.

---

## What is the problem

Every design project reaches a point where you stop gathering and have to say what the
material means — which problem is actually worth solving. That step is the one Design Thinking
never really teaches: Empathize and Ideate have methods, Define has a template.

It used to stay hidden, because gathering and organising took most of the project. AI removed
that work, so interpretation is no longer one difficulty among many — it is the whole
difficulty. And it got harder, not just more visible: there is far more material than before,
and no way to tell where any of it came from. A handwritten note meant you had been in the
room. An AI summary and your own observation look identical on the page.

The result: **teams commit to the first interpretation that sounds reasonable, instead of the
one best supported by what they actually saw.**

---

## Which aspect we are solving

Not the whole process. One step:

> **Turning collected material into a committed problem statement** — and being able to show
> what that commitment rests on.

Concretely, helping a team:
- separate what they **observed** from what they **concluded**
- notice the question they did not ask
- consider more than one explanation before choosing
- record what they decided and why

There is a second, smaller part. Later, when a prototype fails, the record lets them work out
**what to change** — the problem, the idea, or the execution. They can only answer that
because the reasoning was written down earlier.

**Not solving:** planning or running research, generating ideas, building prototypes, or
managing the project.

---

## Which user we are targeting

**Primary:** students who want to start a company — whatever they study. Engineering students
with startup ambitions, MBA students on venture tracks, E-cell members, and final-year students
turning a project into a startup. Defined by the intent to build, not by the degree.

**Secondary:** their mentors — incubator mentors, E-cell advisors, entrepreneurship faculty —
who see the pitch but never the reasoning behind it.

---

## Why that user

**For a startup, the wrong problem is fatal.** In a course, choosing the wrong problem costs a
grade. In a startup, building something nobody needs is how most of them die. Our core problem
stops being a teaching concern and becomes the central risk of the venture.

**They feel the pain and can name it.** Every aspirant asks: *is this a real problem? has someone
already built it? what makes mine different?* The product answers exactly those three.

**They're the most exposed to the average answer.** Ask an AI for a startup idea in a space and
you get the same answer as everyone else who asked — and a generic problem means a crowded
market.

**Engineers who want to found a company have the failure in its purest form.** They were
trained to solve the problem they're given. A founder has to choose the problem, and their
instinct is to jump straight to building.

**It's about intent, not degree.** A plain engineering project isn't business-focused, but an
engineering student who wants to start a company is. That's why the segment is defined by the
ambition, not the programme.

## Pain points

### What students experience
- We have forty pages of notes and we do not know what they mean.
- Everyone on the team read the same interviews differently and we cannot tell who is right.
- We chose this problem because it sounded good, not because we could show why.
- I cannot tell what we actually saw from what we — or the AI — concluded.
- We spent three weeks building before realising the problem was wrong.
- The prototype failed and we do not know whether the idea was bad or the problem was wrong.
- It is due Friday, so we picked something.

Underneath most of these is discomfort. Sitting with forty unresolved observations feels bad,
and choosing an answer makes the discomfort stop. Closing early is often a way of relieving
that, not a reasoning mistake.

### What startup aspirants experience
- I don't know if this is a real problem or I just think it is.
- I found out after two months that someone already built this.
- Every idea I get from AI sounds like every other startup.
- I can't explain why my idea is different when a mentor pushes back.
- I'm scared to pivot because I don't know if the problem was wrong or my solution was.

### What mentors experience
- I see the pitch, never the reasoning behind it, so I can't tell if the problem is real.
- Founders arrive in love with a solution and can't say what evidence supports the problem.
- The same mistakes repeat with every cohort, and I only catch them at demo day.

---

## What the solution is

An iPad app that keeps a **reasoning ledger** for a design project — a running record of what
the team observed, what they concluded, and what each conclusion rests on. It does not do the
thinking. It asks questions and keeps the record.

Four things it does:

**1. Marks where every piece of material came from.**
Each note is labelled: *saw it*, *heard it*, *concluded it*, or *assuming it*. This is the
foundation, because it makes the difference between an observation and an interpretation
visible — which is exactly what gets lost when notes are AI-summarised.

**2. Points at the jump.**
When someone writes a conclusion, the app names the step they skipped: *"You went from 'three
people paused at the form' to 'the form is confusing.' What made you add that?"*
It identifies the gap. It does not fill it.

**3. Requires an alternative before committing.**
You cannot lock in a problem statement without recording at least one other explanation you
considered, and why you set it aside. This is the direct counter to picking the first thing
that sounds reasonable.

**4. Keeps everything linked.**
Each claim stays attached to what it rests on, for the length of the project. When a prototype
fails later, you can find which assumption broke — and therefore whether to change the problem,
the idea, or the execution.

### It is designed to be needed less over time
At first the app asks the questions outright. Later it only says a question is missing.
Eventually it says nothing and just records. **A team needing fewer prompts is the measure that
it is working** — otherwise it would become another crutch, which is the problem we started from.

### What it deliberately does not do
Generate insights, rank ideas, or tell you what the problem is. The moment it does that, it has
taken over the step the team is supposed to be learning.

### The first version is not software
It starts as a paper playbook used with a few teams. If working through the questions on paper
does not change what teams decide, building an app would not change it either.

---

## In one paragraph

Design projects fail at the point where evidence becomes a decision. That step was always the
least-taught part of the process, and AI has made it the only hard part left — while adding
more material and removing any way to tell where that material came from. We are building
something that helps student startup aspirants make that one step deliberately: separating what
they saw from what they concluded, requiring an alternative before they commit, and keeping a
record of the reasoning so that when something later goes wrong, they can tell what to change.
It is an iPad app that asks questions rather than answering them, and that is built to be
needed less as the team gets better.
