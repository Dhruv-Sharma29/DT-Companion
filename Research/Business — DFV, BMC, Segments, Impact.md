# 1. Desirability · Feasibility · Viability

Answer honestly, including where it is weak. A confident three-for-three is not believed.

## Desirability — **split** #key
| | |
|---|---|
| **Students** | **Weak.** The problem is unfelt — they do not know their insight is thin. Nobody searches for this. → [[Why Students Struggle — Causal Factors]] #5 |
| **Instructors** | **Strong.** Acutely felt every semester: they see the wrong turn at the final presentation, too late to help |

> The honest position: **desirability is real but sits with the secondary user.** That is why
> adoption is mandated rather than chosen, and it is a genuine risk, not a detail.
> The [[Backlog]] #13 pitch-assembly feature is the strongest available answer — it gives
> students a selfish reason to maintain the record.

## Feasibility — **strong**
Text, links, and a small on-device model. No hard technical problem. Risks are design risks:
- **Capture overhead** — the failure that killed IBIS. → [[Terminology & Prior Art]]
- **Prompt quality** — a nudge that misses feels stupid and gets dismissed
- Both are testable at stage 0 on paper, before any code

## Viability — **weak as a business, fine as a project**
- Students will not pay. Institutions might — per-course or per-cohort licensing.
- No consumer path. No advertising path.
- **For a course project this is not a failure**, but say what it *would* take: institutional
  licensing, sold to departments, with the instructor as champion.

# 4. User segments, in depth

> [!important] Segment changed 11 Sep 2026 → [[User — Student Startup Aspirants]]. Desirability for
> the primary user moves from **weak** to **strong**: for a founder the wrong problem is fatal,
> and *is it real / is it taken / is it mine?* are questions they already ask.

### A — Student startup aspirants · **primary user**
Any degree, defined by intent to build a company. Engineering students with founder ambitions
are the sharpest sub-segment; MBA venture-track students a strong second.
**Feels:** overwhelmed by material, disagreeing without resolution, deadline pressure.
**Does not feel:** that their reasoning is weak.
**Motivated by:** the grade, and not wasting three weeks. → the pitch-assembly feature.

### B — Instructors · **secondary user, buyer, champion** #key
Teaching 20–60 students across 5–15 teams.
**Feels:** can only assess the artifact; catches wrong turns too late; the same errors annually.
**Wants:** visibility into process, mid-project.
**Can:** mandate a tool, and pay.

### C — Bootcamps and incubators · **later**
Shorter timelines, higher premature-convergence risk, motivated by real outcomes.
Same mechanic, less friction tolerated.

### D — Professional teams · **explicitly not now**
They have judgement and want speed. Friction is a tax. Serving them means a different product;
attempting both is the most likely way to fail. → [[The Automation Paradox]]

# 5. Reducing assumption churn — reframed #key

The ask was *"fewer changes of assumptions."* Be careful: **changing an assumption because
evidence contradicted it is the process working.** A product that reduces that is harmful, and
a DT expert will say so immediately.

The right targets:

| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| fewer assumption changes | fewer **unnoticed** assumptions |
| fewer revisions | fewer **late** reversals |
| more stable decisions | decisions that **hold up when tested** |

**How the product delivers those three:**
1. **Surfacing assumptions early** — a live count of what's untested makes them visible in
   week one rather than week eight.
2. **Cheap early tests** — a visible assumption invites a five-minute check; an invisible one
   gets discovered by a failed prototype.
3. **Alternatives on record** — when a reversal comes, the alternative is already written down,
   so the team pivots in an hour rather than restarting.

> A team using this should change their mind **more often, earlier, and more cheaply.**
> That is the goal, and it is the opposite of what "fewer changes" sounds like. #insight

# 6. Impact

**For a team:** fewer weeks spent building on a wrong problem; disagreement surfaced in week
two instead of at the final presentation; a submission that can be defended under questioning.

**For a cohort:** reasoning becomes visible, therefore gradeable, therefore practised. That
changes the incentive we had written off as untouchable →
[[Why Students Struggle — Causal Factors]] #9.

**For how DT is taught:** the Define stage gets a method, and the missing feedback loop —
factor #12, the deepest structural cause — is partially restored, because a team can finally
see which of their assumptions failed.

**Honest ceiling:** this does not create domain expertise, does not fix institutional
assessment, and cannot make a team care. It makes weak reasoning **visible**. Everything after
that is still up to the people. #key
