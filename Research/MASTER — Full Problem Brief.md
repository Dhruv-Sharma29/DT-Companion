# 📕 MASTER — Full Problem Brief

> [!warning] Partly outdated (13 Sep 2026)
> Written when the user was design/course students with an instructor as buyer. The user is now
> **student startup aspirants**, with mentors and incubators as the channel. For the current
> position use [[Core Statement]] and [[Presentation Prep]]. The argument, terms and DT
> reasoning here are still valid.

**8 Sep 2026** — everything in one place, for defending the problem to a Design Thinking
expert. Detail notes are linked throughout; this is the spine.

---

# 1. The one-minute version

> AI made two Design Thinking activities nearly free — **collecting evidence** and
> **generating ideas**. Both are production. Neither is judgement. So the constraint moved
> downstream to the step that resisted automation: deciding **which reading of the evidence
> deserves the next unit of effort**.
>
> That step was always hard. What changed is that it is now performed on far more material,
> with far weaker provenance, by people who get far fewer reps at it — and in a curriculum
> that never taught it as a method and never grades it.
>
> The result: novice design teams commit to the **first plausible** interpretation instead
> of the **best-supported** one.

---

# 2. The five-minute version

Design Thinking has always had an awkward middle. Empathize has methods — interviews,
shadowing, diary studies. Ideate has methods — Crazy 8s, SCAMPER, brainwriting. Prototype
has methods. **Define does not.** It is taught as an *output to produce* — write a point-of-view
statement — rather than a *procedure to follow*. Students were always thin there.

For a long time that thinness was hidden, because the surrounding work was expensive.
Transcribing interviews, organising notes, generating options — that consumed most of the
project. Effort itself acted as a **grounding signal**: if you wrote the note, you were in
the room, and you knew which claims you had earned.

AI removed that work. Two consequences follow, and they are different.

The first is a **bottleneck shift**, and it is unremarkable — the same thing happened when
digital photography made capture free and curation became the hard part, and when search made
information free and credibility judgement became the hard part.

The second is specific to AI and is the real story: **provenance collapse**. Because effort
no longer tracks grounding, a synthesised claim and an observed one look identical on the
page — both fluent, both confident, both unattributed. Volume rises at the same time, so
there is more material to judge, and each item carries less signal about whether it was
earned. Meanwhile a fluent, well-formed answer arriving early anchors the team and suppresses
the search for a better one.

And the environment perpetuates it. Students are graded on the **artifact, not the reasoning**,
so a polished wrong answer outscores a well-reasoned uncertain one. Projects end at the
prototype, so teams almost never learn whether their interpretation was right — and
**judgement cannot develop where outcomes are never revealed.**

So the problem is not that students are lazy or that AI is bad. It is that the only remaining
hard part of the process is the one part nobody teaches as a method, nobody grades, and nobody
gives feedback on — and it just became the whole job.

# 4. Where exactly this sits in the process

Say this in the expert's own vocabulary:

- **Double Diamond** — the **neck of the first diamond**: the convergence at the end of
  Discover, where research becomes a defined problem.
- **d.school five-stage** — the **Empathize/Observe → Define handoff**.
- **Kolko** — *design synthesis*.
- **Peirce** — the **abductive leap**: inference to the best explanation.

> [!important] Not a stage — a recurring moment.
> Anticipate: *"Design Thinking isn't linear, so your handoff doesn't exist."*
> Correct. The handoff is not a stage boundary; it is **every moment where accumulated
> evidence must become a commitment.** In a non-linear process that happens many times, which
> strengthens the case rather than weakening it.

# 6. The proposed response

The problem stands on its own; this is what follows from it. Expect to be asked, and expect
the response to be judged by whether it obeys the constraints the problem sets.

## What it is
A **reasoning ledger** — a running, dated record of what the team observed, what they
concluded, and what that conclusion rests on — kept by an app that **asks questions instead of
answering them.**

## The three mechanics → [[AI Nudging — Interaction Design]]
1. **Provenance split.** Every entry is forced into *saw / infer / assume*. Attacks provenance
   collapse directly.
2. **The missing question.** The AI names the rung skipped on the Ladder of Inference, or the
   question-move not yet made — up, down, sideways, against. It never climbs the ladder itself.
3. **Fading.** Support is explicit at first, then hints, then silence. **Progress is measured
   as needing less of it.**

## The governing rule #key
> **AI notices. The human commits.**

It may surface contradictions, draft provenance tags, and ask the unasked question. It may
never rank insights, choose the problem, or supply the answer. This is what keeps the response
from being the disease — see [[The Automation Paradox]].

## Scope
- **Start** — *"We have material and don't know what it means."*
- **End** — *"We committed to this problem, here is the evidence, here is what we rejected."*
- **Out** — recruiting, ideation, prototyping, task management.
- **Narrow intervention, wide record**: active support at one handoff; the ledger persists
  across the whole project. → [[Scope, Platform & Users]]

**Across the DT loop** — deep before the prototype, thin at it, a second peak after it. The
mechanic *claim → evidence → alternatives → commitment* fires at three convergences: which
problem (**the product**), which idea (thin), and what did the test disconfirm (**second
peak**). At that third point the ledger locates the broken assumption and so answers the
question teams always stall on — change the problem, the idea, or the execution?
→ [[Process Coverage — How Far the App Helps]]

## For whom
**Student startup aspirants** — defined by the intent to build a company, not by degree.
Engineering students with founder ambitions are the sharpest fit: they must switch from
problem-*solving* to problem-*setting*. For a startup the wrong problem is fatal, so the stake is
the highest possible. **Mentors and incubators** are the secondary user and the channel —
*reasoning becomes fundable*. → [[User — Student Startup Aspirants]]

*(Earlier framing, kept for the course context:)* **The instructor is the buyer** — and more than
that: a ledger makes reasoning *visible, therefore gradeable, therefore worth doing*, which is
the only available lever on factor #11, the one structural cause we otherwise wrote off.

## Platform → [[Platform Decision — iPad App]]
**Native iPad app, iPad only.** Because the promise is *make reasoning visible*, and a chain
of reasoning must be seen whole, by a team, at once — see Q15 and Q16.

## How far to build
| Stage | What | Proves |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Paper playbook, 3 teams, this week | Does it change what they *decide*? |
| 1 | **Wizard of Oz** — a human plays the AI | The interaction, with nothing built |
| 2 | Working prototype, one moment, one team | It works unattended |
| 3 | Pilot in one real course | Outcome difference vs. a control |

**Target stage 2, aim at 3. Not go-to-market.** The differentiator is a learning outcome, and
the only proof of a learning outcome is a cohort comparison.

# 7. Hard questions an expert will ask

### Q1. "Isn't this just synthesis? We've taught that for decades."
Yes — and that's the point. The problem is not new; its **dominance** is. When collection and
ideation consumed most of a project, weak synthesis was a flaw. Now that they consume almost
none, weak synthesis is the entire failure. The activity is old; its share of the difficulty
is new, and so is the material it operates on.

### Q2. "Didn't students always struggle with this?"
Yes. I'm not claiming AI created it. AI **removed the work that hid it, multiplied the
material it operates on, stripped the provenance signal, and eliminated the reps that used to
build the skill.** Cause unchanged; magnitude, shape and visibility changed.

### Q3. "Isn't affinity mapping already the method for Define?" #key
Affinity mapping is a **clustering** method. It tells you what is *similar*. It does not tell
you what is *true*, or which cluster deserves belief. The move from a labelled cluster to an
insight is the **abductive leap**, and that leap is exactly what is unmethoded. Affinity
mapping also assumes each note carries its own provenance — which is precisely what
AI-generated notes destroy.

### Q4. "Design is abductive. Demanding evidence for a creative leap will kill creativity." #key
The most serious objection. Take it seriously:

I am not requiring proof **before** the leap. Abduction is legitimate and necessary — you
cannot get an insight by deduction from field notes. What I require is that the leap be
**visible and labelled as a leap**. The failure mode is not abduction; it is **unacknowledged
abduction masquerading as observation** — a team believing they *saw* something they in fact
*inferred*. The ledger does not forbid the jump. It records that you jumped, so you can
revisit it when the prototype fails. And the support **fades** as judgement develops, so it
constrains novices, not experts.

### Q5. "This is design rationale. IBIS and Compendium failed. Why won't you?"
The honest answer: **capture overhead killed them.** Recording rationale cost more than it
returned, so people stopped. That is still my largest risk. Three mitigations:
AI **drafts** the capture (the exact cost that killed IBIS); the record is a **byproduct** of
prompts the team wanted anyway, not a separate chore; and the instructor's assessment supplies
an **extrinsic reason** to keep it current. If capture cost isn't beaten, the project fails —
and that's the thing to test at stage 0. → [[Terminology & Prior Art]]

### Q6. "Why won't they just use ChatGPT?" #key
Because an LLM returns the **most probable** answer — the consensus of its training data — and
the most probable answer is by definition the **least unique**. Twenty teams researching similar
areas get twenty variations of the same problem statement. LLMs are convergence machines, and
premature convergence is the failure this project is about.

Uniqueness lives where the model can't reach: in the team's own **first-hand anomaly** — the
observation that doesn't fit the generic story — and in the **gap in the landscape** where
existing solutions stop. *Uniqueness = your anomaly × the landscape's gap.* The companion
surfaces the first, maps the second, and never generates the angle itself — a model generating
"unique insights" just produces the average of unique-sounding ones.
→ [[Why Not ChatGPT — The Average Answer]] · [[Landscape Intel]]

### Q7. "Isn't premature convergence sometimes correct? Satisficing under deadline is rational."
Agreed, and the goal is not to prevent convergence. It is to make convergence **deliberate**
rather than accidental — you may commit early, but knowingly, having seen what you're passing
up. A team that converges fast with alternatives on record has done something different from
a team that never saw the alternatives.

### Q8. "You're solving a problem students don't know they have."
True, and that's why **awareness is the binding constraint** before method. It's also why the
buyer is the instructor and adoption is mandated rather than chosen. A tool for an unfelt
problem must arrive through someone who feels it. → [[Scope, Platform & Users]]

### Q9. "Aren't you building the very thing you're criticising — more AI in the process?"
The distinction is strict: **AI notices, the human commits.** It may surface contradictions,
draft provenance tags, and ask the question that wasn't asked. It may never rank insights,
choose the problem, or supply the answer. And it **fades** — the measure of success is the
user needing less of it over time. → [[The Automation Paradox]]

### Q10. "How would you measure success?"
- **Fading rate** — how quickly the user stops needing prompts (the headline metric)
- Alternatives considered before commitment
- Provenance accuracy — can they correctly mark observed vs. inferred?
- Reframes triggered by evidence rather than by opinion
- Instructor-scored **reasoning quality**, independent of artifact polish
- Stage 3: cohort comparison against a control group

### Q11. "Which Design Thinking model are you working in?"
Double Diamond for locating the problem — it is the only common model that names *convergence*
as a distinct act, which is exactly my subject. d.school's five stages for talking to students,
since that's what they're taught. Dorst's frame creation and Schön's reflective practice for
the iteration half.

### Q12. "Novices can't recognise a good question. So how does prompting help?"
That is the reason the AI **supplies** the question at level 1 rather than asking them to
generate one. The tool is an **external standard** until an internal one forms, then it
withdraws. That is standard scaffolding-and-fading, not a workaround.

### Q13. "What about domain expertise? A novice can't know what matters in a hospital."
Correct, and I don't claim to fix it — it takes years. It is a stated **limit** on the
intervention. What the tool can do is make the *absence* of a baseline visible, so the team
knows to go and ask an expert instead of confidently inventing significance.

### Q14. "What's your evidence for any of this?"
None yet, and I won't pretend otherwise. Here are the four assumptions and the tests, and the
one I can run this week for free. → §5.

### Q15. "Why an iPad app? Reasoning tools are usually desktop software." #key
Because the promise is **make reasoning visible**, and that sets two requirements a phone and a
laptop each fail differently. A claim, the evidence under it, the alternatives rejected and the
assumption still untested form a **structure** — read one card at a time it is not seen at all,
so the surface needs area. And the payoff moment is a **team** looking at its own argument
together: [[AI Nudging — Interaction Design]] specifies silent divergence then collision, and a
collision is a shared-viewing event. Four people gather around an iPad on a table. Nobody
gathers around a phone, and a laptop puts one person behind the screen and everyone else in
front of it. The core interaction is also literally two panes — claim beside its warrant, note
12 beside note 31 — and **comparison requires simultaneity**, which is what stops an
unsupported claim slipping past.

### Q16. "Why native at all? A website could do this." #key
The weakest link, so answer it by inverting the question: **what could the product no longer
promise as a website?** Four things, and everything else is convenience.

**1. On-device AI — the load-bearing one.** A web app must send interview material to a server;
an iPad app processes it on the device. This is not a privacy feature, it is a **permission**
difference. Design research involves real named people — patients, children, vulnerable users.
Ship that to a server and the consent form hardens, the ethics review hardens, and students
**self-censor what they put in**. A sanitised paraphrase of an interview is exactly the
provenance-stripped text this project exists to fight. **A tool that cannot hold the real data
cannot teach judgement about the real data.**

**2. Pencil at native latency.** Annotation *is* reasoning work — circling the weak link,
marking the sentence a claim rests on. The web has pointer events, not ~9ms latency, pressure
and Scribble. And handwriting is slower than typing, which a product that wants deliberate
friction should welcome.

**3. Storage that can't be silently evicted.** The ledger is a semester-long record. Browser
storage on iOS can be cleared by the OS or by tracking prevention, quietly. A record that can
vanish is not a record — and the alternative, a server, returns you to point 1.

**4. Complete offline function.** Fieldwork happens in wards, factories and basements, and
iOS has the weakest offline-web support of any major platform.

What I will **not** claim: better performance, nicer UI, push notifications, camera access —
the web has all of those, and claiming them would undermine the four that hold.

What the web genuinely wins: distribution, reach beyond Apple devices, ship speed, and the
**instructor's grading view** — which is desktop-shaped and belongs to a different user. The
clean resolution is an iPad app for the team and a small web view for the instructor.

And if the honest answer in context is *native because it is the brief*, say that first.
Retrofitting product logic onto a constraint is what actually gets punished.
→ [[Native vs Web — The Differentiators]]

### Q17. "If the playbook works, why does this need to be software at all?" #key
Fair, and the playbook genuinely comes first — it is the experiment that decides whether the
software deserves to exist. Software earns its place only where paper structurally cannot go:
**capturing provenance at the moment of observation**, **delivering a nudge at the moment of
commitment**, and **holding forty items at once** so contradictions between note 12 and note 31
can be surfaced. If the paper version changes nothing, I will not build the app — and I would
rather find that out in week one than in month four.


### Q18. "Design Thinking is a loop. Does your tool stop at Define?" #key
No — the *coaching* concentrates at Define, but the *record* runs the whole loop, and it pays
off a second time at **Iterate**. When a prototype fails, the question teams stall on is
whether to change the problem, the idea, or the execution. The ledger answers it mechanically:
the test disconfirmed an assumption; if that assumption lives in the problem definition the
frame was wrong, if in the idea the frame holds but the response didn't, if in the execution
both hold and it was built badly. Nobody can do that trace without a record of what the idea
rested on — which is exactly what teams don't keep.

That is also the honest reason the ledger is worth maintaining. Its value at Define is
immediate but modest. Its value at Iterate is what makes keeping it *rational*.

### Q19. "So you're discarding the rest of Design Thinking?" #key
No. Distinguish the **process we work inside** — the full DT loop, unchanged — from the **step
we add support to**. Students still interview, observe, cluster, ideate, prototype and test in
the normal way with the normal methods; our input is whatever they gathered, and our output is
a POV statement, which is what DT asks for anyway. Affinity mapping still happens, and we pick
up after it: clustering tells you what is *similar*, and we handle the move from a cluster to a
claim you can defend. We also depend on the loop — the Iterate payoff in Q18 only exists
because DT loops back.

What we add is what DT does not have: a **method** for the interpretation step, borrowed from
outside the field — the Ladder of Inference, Toulmin's argument structure, provenance tracking.
We are not replacing Design Thinking and not reinventing it. **We are adding the missing method
to the one stage that never had one.**

# 8. Terms to be able to define on demand #key

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| **Sensemaking** | Turning ambiguous material into a workable account of what is going on |
| **Design synthesis** | The DT-specific act of turning research into an insight and a frame (Kolko) |
| **Abduction** | Inference to the best explanation; design's characteristic logic (Peirce) |
| **Frame / reframing** | The lens that determines what counts as the problem (Dorst) |
| **Problem setting** | Choosing which problem to solve, as distinct from solving it (Schön) |
| **Reflection-in-action** | Adjusting your approach while the work is happening (Schön) |
| **Wicked problem** | No definitive formulation, no stopping rule, no right answer (Rittel & Webber) |
| **Provenance** | Where a claim came from and what it rests on |
| **Ladder of Inference** | Data → selection → meaning → assumption → conclusion → action (Argyris) |
| **Toulmin model** | Claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal |
| **Scaffolding & fading** | Support given, then deliberately removed as competence grows |
| **Metacognition** | Thinking about your own thinking — planning, monitoring, evaluating it |
| **Cognitive offloading** | Delegating mental work to an external aid |
| **Premature convergence** | Committing to an answer before the alternatives were examined |

# 9. Weaknesses to own before they're found #key

Raise these yourself. It converts an attack into a demonstration of rigour.

1. **No primary evidence yet.** Four assumptions, four tests, none run.
2. **Capture overhead** is the historical killer of every rationale system. Unsolved.
3. **Domain expertise** is untouchable by this intervention.
4. **Assessment structure** is institutional; the tool only makes reasoning gradeable, it
   cannot make anyone grade it.
5. **The problem is unfelt by the primary user**, which is a real adoption risk.
6. **"AI harms critical thinking"** is contested and correlational — so the argument is built
   on ratio-shift and provenance instead, which are structural and don't need that literature.
7. **Friction is a hard sell.** Deliberately slowing people down needs the fading story to be
   convincing, or it reads as a worse tool.
8. **iPad narrows reach and weakens two claims.** Not every student owns one. Field capture is
   more awkward than on a phone, and the nudge-at-the-moment mechanic largely goes — an iPad is
   not in your pocket. **Do not claim in-the-moment nudges.** → [[Platform Decision — iPad App]]

# 10. If there are thirty seconds left

> Design Thinking's weakest-taught stage just became its only hard one. AI made evidence and
> ideas free, but it can't tell you which of them deserves your next two weeks — and it
> removed the effort that used to prove you'd earned the answer. I want to build the thing
> that makes a team's reasoning visible enough to defend, and then gets out of the way.

Then, if they ask what it is: *an app that asks the question you didn't ask, records what your
answer rests on, and needs you less every week.*
