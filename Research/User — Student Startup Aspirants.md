# 🚀 User — Student Startup Aspirants

**11 Sep 2026.** Primary user: **students who want to start a company** — whatever they study.
Refines and replaces the earlier MBA-only definition.

## Defined by intent, not by degree #key
The earlier cut was *MBA students*. That was a demographic proxy for the real trait. The trait
is **wanting to build a startup** — and it cuts across programmes:

| Included | Why |
|---|---|
| BTech / engineering students with startup ambitions | The original objection was that engineering *projects* aren't business-focused. A startup aspirant is business-focused **by intent**, whatever the syllabus says |
| MBA students on venture tracks | Still a strong sub-segment — competing in B-plan and pitch competitions |
| E-cell members, hackathon teams, incubation applicants | Already acting on the intent |
| Final-year students turning a project into a company | The most acute moment of all |

**Excluded:** students doing a project only because it's required, with no intent to build
anything from it. They have no stake in whether the problem is right.

## Why this user

### 1. The stake is the highest it can be #key
For a course project, choosing the wrong problem costs a grade. **For a startup, choosing the
wrong problem is how it dies.** Building something nobody needs is widely reported as the most
common reason startups fail. That makes our core problem — committing to the first plausible
problem instead of the evidence-backed one — the central risk of the whole venture, not a
teaching concern.
> [!warning] Don't quote a failure statistic until it's sourced — see [[Sources]]. #todo #source

### 2. The pain is felt, and named
Every aspirant asks the same three questions, unprompted:
- *Is this a real problem, or do I just think it is?*
- *Has someone already built this?*
- *What makes mine different?*

Those map one-to-one onto the product: the ledger, [[Landscape Intel]], and
[[Why Not ChatGPT — The Average Answer]]. Desirability goes from **weak** (course students
who didn't feel it) to **strong**.

### 3. They're the most exposed to the average answer
An aspirant asking ChatGPT for a startup idea in a given space gets the same answer as every
other aspirant who asked. In a startup, **a generic problem means a crowded market.**

### 4. Engineers carry the purest form of the failure #insight
Engineering training rewards solving the problem you're handed. An engineer who wants to found
a company must suddenly **choose** the problem — Schön's *problem setting*. Their instinct is to
jump to building: premature convergence, well practised. The sharpest sub-segment.

### 5. They already speak a compatible language
Startup aspirants learn **Lean Startup** and **customer discovery** alongside or instead of
Design Thinking. That vocabulary maps directly onto ours:

| Their term | Our term |
|---|---|
| Customer discovery / *get out of the building* | **Observation** vs. inference — first-hand evidence |
| Riskiest assumption | **Open assumptions**, ranked |
| Problem–solution fit hypothesis | **Commitment** |
| **Pivot or persevere** | **Trace → break point** |
| Competitive analysis / *is it already done?* | [[Landscape Intel]] |

> [!important] *"Change the problem, the idea, or the execution?"* is literally the
> **pivot-or-persevere** decision. The product answers the question every founder dreads, with
> a record instead of a gut feeling. #insight

Speak their language in the product and they recognise it instantly. → [[Product Lexicon]]

## Who else is involved

| Role | Who | Their pain |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary user** | Student startup aspirants, solo or small teams | *Is this real, is it taken, is it mine?* |
| **Secondary user** | **Mentors** — incubator mentors, E-cell advisors, entrepreneurship faculty | They see the pitch, never the reasoning — the same pain the instructor had |
| **Buyer / channel** | College incubators, E-cells, university entrepreneurship centres, accelerators, startup-competition organisers | Want more fundable, better-validated founders coming through |
| **Later** | Early-stage founders outside college | Same need, less friction tolerance |
| **Not now** | Students with no venture intent | No stake in whether the problem is right |

### What changed about the buyer #key
The earlier model leaned on **the instructor** — the person who could mandate the tool and
whose grading made reasoning worth doing. A startup aspirant may have no instructor.
The replacement is the **mentor and the incubator**:
- they face the identical pain — seeing the pitch, not the reasoning behind it
- incubators screen applicants and would value a founder who arrives with a **reasoning ledger**
- *"Apply with your ledger"* is a strong channel: it makes the record the entry ticket

The "reasoning becomes gradeable" argument survives as **"reasoning becomes fundable"** — a
mentor or incubator can see *why* a founder believes their problem is real.

## What this puts under pressure #question
- **Device** — student founders are laptop-first. Re-check iPad ownership before building.
  → [[Platform Decision — iPad App]]
- **Friction tolerance** — aspirants want speed. Concentrated friction only
  ([[Making It Attractive]]), and they'll tolerate it when it visibly improves their odds.
- **No mandate** — without an instructor requiring it, adoption must be earned. Mock Crit and
  landscape intel have to be good enough that aspirants choose it.
- **Solo founders** — many aspirants work alone, so team features (divergence round,
  collision) matter less for part of the segment.

## Updated problem statement
> AI gives every aspiring founder the same average answer. So student startup aspirants commit
> to the first problem that sounds right — often one already crowded — instead of the
> differentiated one their own evidence supports. In a startup, the wrong problem is fatal.
