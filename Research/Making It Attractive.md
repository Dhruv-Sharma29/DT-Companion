# ✨ Making It Attractive

**11 Sep 2026.** How to make students *want* to use a tool whose core act is pointing at
weak reasoning.

## Why this is unusually hard #key
Two facts from earlier work collide:
1. **The problem is unfelt.** Students don't know their insight is thin, so nobody goes looking
   for a fix. → [[Business — DFV, BMC, Segments, Impact]] (desirability: weak for students)
2. **The product is deliberately slow.** Friction at commitment is the design, not a bug.
   → [[The Automation Paradox]]

A tool for an unfelt problem that slows you down is the hardest thing in the world to make
appealing. So the strategy cannot be "make them want the problem solved."

> [!important] The principle #key
> **Sell the pain they feel. Deliver the benefit they don't feel as a side effect.**

What students *do* feel: overwhelm at the material, team arguments, deadline panic, and — most
of all — **dread of being questioned at the final presentation.**

---

## The trap: gamification #key
Points, badges, streaks and leaderboards all backfire here, for specific reasons:

| Mechanic | What students would actually do |
|---|---|
| **Points per entry** | Log junk to earn points. Rewards *volume* — the exact thing AI made cheap |
| **Streaks** | Open the app daily for no reason. The work is episodic, so the streak is fake |
| **Team leaderboards** | Hide uncertainty to look good — the **opposite** of what we need them to do |
| **A reasoning score** | Optimise the score instead of the reasoning, and argue with the grader |

> **Anything you reward, students will produce.** So only reward what cannot be faked
> cheaply. Almost nothing countable passes that test. #insight

---

## What actually works — ranked

### 1. Mock Crit — find the weak spots before the teacher does #key
The best idea in this note, and it comes straight out of design culture.

In design education, a **crit** is where you present and get questioned. Students know it and
dread it. The ledger already knows every unwarranted leap, every open assumption, every
rejected alternative. So:

> **Mock Crit** — the app questions *your* project, using *your* weak links, before the
> instructor does.

Why it works so well:
- **It sells the felt pain** — fear of the crit — not the unfelt one.
- It **flips the emotional charge** of the entire product. Finding a weak link stops being a
  criticism and becomes a *service*: *better you found it than the panel.*
- It is **pure questioning**, never answering — fully consistent with the thesis.
- It gives students a **selfish reason to keep the ledger honest**: the crit is only as good
  as the record it draws from.
- It turns the moment before the deadline — the moment they currently close early — into the
  moment they look hardest.

### 2. The first five minutes must pay off before any friction #key
Drop forty messy notes in. Within seconds: sorted into observed / secondhand / inferred, with
counts. *"72% of your material is secondhand."*

That is relief from the overwhelm **and** a genuine insight, with zero effort from them. The
"aha" must arrive **before** the app asks anything difficult. Friction earns tolerance only
after value has been delivered.

### 3. Your final pitch, assembled from work you already did
Already identified as the strongest adoption argument → [[Backlog]] #13. The felt payoff: a
week saved at the end of the semester. Lead the onboarding with it.

### 4. Concentrate the friction — remove it everywhere else #key
Friction in one place feels **intentional**. Friction everywhere feels like a **bad app**.
- Capture: effortless — share sheet, camera, three taps.
  → [[Native vs Web — The Differentiators]]
- Sorting: one tap per item.
- **Commitment: this is the only place it slows you down.**

### 5. Make the argument visible as it grows
The iPad case rests on reasoning being *seen* ([[Platform Decision — iPad App]]). Make that
satisfying: a claim with five observations beneath it looks solid; a claim with none visibly
**floats**. Visual weight, not a score — and it is harder to game, because it is just a view
of real links that teammates and the instructor can inspect.

### 6. Make disagreement a reveal, not a fight
After a divergence round, the collision view is a *reveal*: *"Here's where you four read the
same interview differently."* Framed as curiosity, the most dreaded team moment becomes the
most interesting one.

### 7. Reward graduation, not activity #key
The one reward that aligns perfectly with the goal:
> *"This week you asked three of the questions the app used to ask you."*

It celebrates **needing the tool less**. It cannot be faked by using the app more. It is the
fading mechanic made visible and made to feel like progress. → [[Teaching Model]]

### 8. The "saved you three weeks" moment
When a trace finds a broken assumption, show *when it was recorded*: *"You flagged this as
untested in week 2."* Dramatise the payoff. This is the moment that converts a sceptic.

## Voice — this matters more than any feature #key
The product's core act is pointing at weakness. If it sounds like red ink, they close it.

| Not this | This |
|---|---|
| "Warning: unsupported claim" | "Interesting — what made you connect these?" |
| "Error: no evidence" | "This one's floating. What did you see?" |
| "You must add an alternative" | "What else could explain it?" |
| "Score: 6/10" | *(never)* |

The voice of a **sharp, curious friend** — never a grader.

---

## Adoption path
Instructor-led first session: the whole class imports material together and runs a Mock Crit
on a sample project. Mandate plus a shared first win, instead of sixty students discovering it
alone. → [[Scope, Platform & Users]]
