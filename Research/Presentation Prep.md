# Part 1 — The story in 5 beats

Everything you say tomorrow hangs off this.

```
1  SHIFT     AI made research and ideas cheap, and everyone gets the same ones
2  PROBLEM   So founders build on untested assumptions and pick the wrong problem
3  USER      College students who want to start a company
4  GAP       Mentors and incubators can't reach most of them, and ChatGPT gives the average answer
5  SOLUTION  An iPad companion that keeps their reasoning honest: it asks, it doesn't answer
```

### 1 · The shift
AI made two things nearly free: **collecting information** and **generating ideas**. But it
gives everyone the **same** ideas, because a model returns the most probable answer, and the
most probable answer is the least unique.

### 2 · The problem
Student founders' research ends up scattered across notes, chats and AI tools. They don't have
a clear path from research to a decision. And they lose track of **what they actually know
versus what they're assuming**. So they rely on generic AI answers, build on untested
assumptions, and often find out too late that the problem was never real.
**43% of failed startups had poor product-market fit** (CB Insights, March 2026).

### 3 · The user
**College students who want to build a startup.** They're defined by intent, not by degree.
- **Engineers especially.** They're trained to *solve* the problem they're given. A founder
  has to *choose* the problem.
- **Tier 2–3 cities especially**, where mentors are scarce.
- For them the wrong problem isn't a bad grade. **It kills the startup.**

### 4 · The gap
- **32.5%** of Indian college students are already nascent entrepreneurs, but India has only
  **~0.8 incubators per million people**, against 8–10 in the US, UK and China.
- **48%** of incubators are in Tier-I cities, while **over half** of startups now come from
  Tier-II/III.
- **ChatGPT** gives the average answer. **Miro and Notion** store material but don't record
  reasoning. **Lean Startup books** teach the method but aren't there while you work.

### 5 · The solution
An **iPad app** that keeps a **reasoning ledger**: what the founder saw, what they concluded,
and what each conclusion rests on.
1. **Marks every note**: *saw it · heard it secondhand · concluded it · assuming it*
2. **Asks the missing question**: *"what made you jump from this to that?"*
3. **Requires an alternative** before they commit to a problem
4. **Landscape Intel**: what already exists and where it stops, always with sources
5. **Trace**: when a test fails, it shows whether to change the problem, the idea or the
   execution. That's the **pivot-or-persevere** decision.
6. **Mock Crit**: questions their weak spots before a mentor or investor does

**The rule: AI notices, the founder decides.** It never ranks ideas or hands over the answer.
And it's built to be **needed less over time**.

### Remember three words: **real · taken · theirs**
> *Is my problem **real**? Has someone already **taken** it? What makes it **theirs**?*
> The ledger answers *real*, Landscape Intel answers *taken*, and anomaly-surfacing answers
> *theirs*.

# Part 3 — Words to know

| Term                         | One sentence                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reasoning ledger**         | A running record of what you saw, what you concluded, and what each conclusion rests on             |
| **Observation vs inference** | What you saw or heard, versus what you concluded from it. *The one distinction everything rests on* |
| **Secondhand**               | Material you didn't witness yourself: a teammate's summary, a report, an AI answer                  |
| **Assumption**               | Something you're treating as true that nothing supports yet                                         |
| **Warrant**                  | The link that explains *why* your evidence supports your conclusion (Toulmin)                       |
| **Unwarranted leap**         | A conclusion with no evidence attached. The app points it out                                       |
| **Ladder of Inference**      | Data → what you noticed → meaning → assumption → conclusion → action (Argyris)                      |
| **Abduction**                | Reasoning to the best explanation. It's how design reaches insights                                 |
| **Problem setting**          | Choosing *which* problem to solve, as opposed to solving it (Schön)                                 |
| **Premature convergence**    | Committing to an answer before you've looked at the alternatives                                    |
| **Fading**                   | Support that deliberately drops away as the user improves                                           |
| **Pivot or persevere**       | The Lean Startup decision to change direction or keep going                                         |
| **Double Diamond**           | Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver. We sit at the **Define** narrowing                           |

# Part 4 — Questions and answers
 
**How to answer:** give the answer, then one reason, then stop. Don't cover everything. If it
fits, bring it back to *real · taken · theirs*.

## Problem

**Q: What's the problem, in one line?**
AI gives every aspiring founder the same average answer, so student founders commit to the first problem that sounds right instead of the one their own evidence supports.

**Q: Isn't this just organising information? Why not use Notion?**
Notion stores notes. It won't tell you that a claim has no evidence behind it, or that half your
research is secondhand. The scattered information is the *cause*. The actual problem is not
knowing what you know versus what you're assuming.

**Q: Founders always struggled with this. What's new?**
AI didn't create it. Two things changed. Research and ideas are now nearly free, so judgement is
the only hard part left. And AI-generated notes look just like real observations, so founders
can't tell what they actually witnessed. The problem got bigger and harder to see.

**Q: What evidence do you have that this problem exists?**
Secondary evidence: 43% of failed startups had poor product-market fit for lack of market need, and support hasn't kept up with
student entrepreneurship (0.8 incubators per million, most of them in Tier-I cities).
**I haven't run primary research with users yet.** My next step is interviewing student founders
and giving them their own notes to mark "saw it" or "assumed it".
*(Say this plainly. It's the question where honesty scores highest.)*

**Q: Where does this sit in Design Thinking?**
At the narrowing of the first diamond, where research turns into a defined problem. The record
carries on through the whole loop, and it pays off a second time at iteration, when a test fails
and you have to decide what to change.

**Q: Design Thinking isn't linear. Doesn't a "Define stage" contradict that?**
It isn't a stage. It's **every moment where evidence has to become a decision**, and in a
non-linear process that happens many times. That makes the case stronger, not weaker.

## User

**Q: Why startup aspirants and not all students?**
For a course project, the wrong problem costs a grade. For a startup, it's usually why the
company dies. So they have the most at stake, and they already ask *is it real, is it taken, is
it mine*.

**Q: Why engineers in particular?**
Engineering trains you to solve the problem you're given. A founder has to choose the problem.
Engineers jump straight to building, which is the exact habit this addresses.

**Q: Why tier 2–3 cities?**
Over half of startups now come from there, but 48% of incubators are in Tier-I cities. Where there
are no mentors, the app asks the questions a mentor would. Where there are mentors, it lets them
see the founder's reasoning.

**Q: What about solo founders?**
The ledger, the prompts, Landscape Intel and Mock Crit all work for one person. Only the
team-comparison feature needs a team.

## Solution

**Q: What is it, in one line?**
An iPad app that keeps a record of what a founder's conclusions rest on, and asks the question
they didn't ask.

**Q: Why wouldn't they just use ChatGPT?**
ChatGPT returns the most probable answer, so twenty founders asking about the same space get twenty
versions of the same idea. What's unique is in two places a model can't see: the founder's own
**surprising observation**, and the **gap where existing solutions stop**.
*Uniqueness comes from your own anomaly plus the gap in the landscape.*

**Q: You're adding AI to a problem AI caused. Isn't that contradictory?**
There's a strict line. **AI notices, the founder decides.** It can point out contradictions and
ask questions. It never ranks ideas, picks the problem or gives the answer. And it's designed to be
needed less over time.

**Q: Won't a tool that slows you down annoy people?**
It only slows you down at one point: when you commit to a problem. Getting material in takes a
few taps. And Mock Crit gives founders a reason to want the questions, because it's better to find
the weak spot before a mentor does.

**Q: What if a founder's notes are already AI summaries?**
The app asks "were you there?" for each item. Anything they didn't witness is marked secondhand.
If 90% of the notes are secondhand, that's a finding in itself: go and talk to real people before
building.

**Q: Can the competitor feature make things up?**
That's the main risk, so it only shows competitors found by live search, each with a link. Each
one is stored as secondhand and dated. And only the general topic leaves the device, never the
founder's interview data.

**Q: Why not include affinity mapping?**
Miro and paper already do it well, and grouping notes isn't the gap. The gap is getting from a
group of notes to a claim you can defend. We import groupings; we don't build a canvas.

**Q: Does this replace Design Thinking or mentors?**
Neither. Design Thinking has no method for the step from evidence to a decision, and this adds one.
It also brings a mentor's questions to founders who don't have a mentor.

## Platform

**Q: Why an app and not a website?**
The advantages are all where the product meets the device and the real world:
- **On-device AI**, so interview data never leaves the iPad, and founders can safely put real
  interviews in
- The **share sheet**, so material comes in from any app in a few taps
- **Camera and Pencil**, for photographing and marking up real evidence
- **Storage that isn't silently wiped**, and full **offline** use

*What the web does better: distribution. That's fine; a mentor's view could be a website.*

**Q: Why iPad and not iPhone?**
A chain of reasoning has to be seen as a whole. A team can gather around one iPad, and a claim can
sit next to its evidence. A phone shows one card at a time.
*What it costs:* student founders mostly use laptops, so iPad ownership still needs checking.

## Business and feasibility

**Q: Desirability, feasibility, viability?**
- **Desirability:** strong. Founders already worry about whether the problem is real and whether
  it's taken.
- **Feasibility:** strong. It's text, links and a small on-device model. The real risks are
  design risks: how much effort it takes to add material, and how good the prompts are.
- **Viability:** unproven. The likely model is licensing to incubators, E-Cells and
  entrepreneurship centres.

**Q: Who pays?**
Incubators and entrepreneurship centres, who want better-validated founders coming through. One
channel is having founders **apply with their ledger**. It's a hypothesis I haven't tested yet.

**Q: What's your market size?**
*Don't make up a total market figure.* "I haven't sized it rigorously yet. The users are college
startup aspirants, and 32.5% of Indian college students already count as nascent entrepreneurs.
The 1,100+ incubators are a ready channel to reach them. A proper estimate is my next step."

**Q: Who are the competitors?**
ChatGPT, Miro/Notion, research tools like Dovetail, and Lean Startup canvases. **The real
competitor is ChatGPT plus a notes app**, because it's free and already open. None of them track
what your conclusions rest on.

**Q: How would you measure success?**
Founders need fewer prompts over time. Assumptions get tested before building. Alternatives are
considered before committing. They pivot earlier and more cheaply. Mentors rate their reasoning
as stronger.

**Q: How will you test it?**
Three steps, cheapest first:
1. A **paper version** with a few founder teams
2. **Wizard of Oz**, where a person plays the AI
3. A prototype of **one moment**

If the paper version doesn't change what founders decide, an app won't either.

**Q: What's your biggest risk?**
Friction when adding material. It's what killed earlier reasoning-capture tools. The share sheet
and quick capture are the answer, and the paper test checks it first.

# Part 5 — Weak points to raise yourself

Raising these before you're asked shows you've thought it through.

1. **No primary user research yet.** Only secondary data.
2. **Adoption.** There's no instructor to make founders use it, so the app has to be good enough
   that they choose it.
3. **Friction when adding material** has killed similar tools before.
4. **iPad ownership** among student founders hasn't been checked.
5. **Limits.** It can't give founders domain expertise, and it can't make them care. It makes
   weak reasoning *visible*.

# Part 7 — How to answer a question you didn't prepare for

You can't prepare for every question. You can prepare **a method**: work out what *kind* of
question it is, reach for the idea that answers it, and use the answer shape that fits.

## Step 1 — The universal answer shape

```
ANSWER     say it directly, in one sentence
BECAUSE    give one reason
LIMIT      name what it doesn't cover (only if there's a real one)
BACK       link to the core problem, then stop
```

Example: *"Why iPad?"* → **Answer:** because reasoning has to be seen as a whole.
**Because:** a team can gather around one screen and see a claim next to its evidence.
**Limit:** student founders mostly use laptops, which we still need to check.
**Back:** being able to see the reasoning is the whole point of the product.

## Step 2 — Six ideas that answer most questions #key

Almost any question comes back to one of these. When a question surprises you, ask
yourself: *which of the six is this really about?*

| # | Core idea | Use it for questions about… |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **AI notices, the founder decides.** It never gives the answer, and it's needed less over time | AI, automation, "isn't this just ChatGPT", ethics, dependence |
| **2** | **Know vs assume.** Every note is marked saw / secondhand / concluded / assumed | features, data, what makes it different, messy input, honesty |
| **3** | **The average answer.** A model gives everyone the same idea; uniqueness comes from your own observation plus the gap in the market | competitors, ChatGPT, differentiation, why now |
| **4** | **The wrong problem is fatal.** 43% of failed startups had poor product-market fit for lack of market need, and mentors can't reach most founders | why this user, why it matters, who pays, impact, market |
| **5** | **Friction only at commitment.** Adding material is quick; the app slows you down only when you decide | usability, adoption, "won't it annoy people" |
| **6** | **Cheapest test first.** Paper version → a person playing the AI → prototype | evidence, validation, next steps, feasibility, risk |

## Step 3 — Recognise the type of question

| If they ask… | It's a… | Answer shape |
|---|---|---|
| "What is / explain…" | **Clarifying** question | One line, then one concrete example |
| "Why did you choose…" | **Justifying** question | The choice, the reason, what you gave up |
| "Why not just use X / isn't X doing this?" | **Comparison** question | What X does well, what X can't do, the one thing only you do |
| "What if… / what about…" | **Edge-case** question | "Good case." How the product handles it, then the limit |
| "What's your evidence / numbers?" | **Proof** question | What you have, what you don't, how you'll get it |
| "Isn't that a contradiction / flaw?" | **Challenge** | Agree with the part that's true, then draw the distinction, then name the remaining risk |
| "How will you… / what's next?" | **Execution** question | The cheapest test first, and what result would change your mind |
| Something you really don't know | **Out of scope** | "I haven't worked that out yet. Here's how I'd find out." |

**For challenges, agree with the part that's true first.** An examiner pushing back usually
wants to see how you react. Saying "that's a fair point, and here's the distinction" shows
you're in control. Defending everything shows you're not.

## Step 4 — Worked examples of questions that aren't in Part 4

**"What if founders just lie in their ledger?"** → *edge case* · ideas 2 + 1
> Good case. The ledger is for them, so faking it only fools themselves, and Mock Crit or a
> mentor will find the gaps. The limit is that it can't force honesty. It makes weak reasoning
> visible to anyone who looks.

**"How is this different from just having a mentor?"** → *comparison* · idea 4
> A mentor is better when you have one. Most student founders don't: India has about 0.8
> incubators per million people, mostly in big cities. Where there's a mentor, the app shows
> them the founder's reasoning. Where there isn't, it asks the questions a mentor would.

**"What about privacy? These are interviews with real people."** → *challenge* · idea 1 + platform
> It's a real concern, which is why the AI runs on the iPad. Interview data never leaves the
> device. Only the general topic goes online, and only for competitor search.

**"Won't AI be good enough to do all this in two years?"** → *challenge* · ideas 3 + 1
> AI will get better at answering. That makes the problem worse, because everyone will get even
> more polished versions of the same answer. We're not competing on answering. We're making
> sure the founder's own judgement grows.

**"Why would anyone pay?"** → *justifying* · idea 4
> Incubators and entrepreneurship centres want better-validated founders coming through. A
> founder who arrives with a reasoning ledger has shown why their problem is real. That's the
> hypothesis, and I haven't tested it yet.

**"What happens after the Define stage?"** → *clarifying* · idea 2
> The record carries on through the whole project. When a prototype fails, the app traces it
> back to the assumption that broke, which tells you whether to change the problem, the idea or
> the execution.

**"Isn't this too complicated for beginners?"** → *challenge* · idea 5
> The complexity is hidden. A beginner sees one question at a time, and only at the moment they
> commit. The support is strongest at the start and fades as they improve.

## Step 5 — Practise it
- Get a friend to ask **anything** — the stranger the better. For each question, name the type
  and the core idea out loud *before* answering. After ten questions, this becomes automatic.
- **Time yourself: 30 seconds per answer.** If you run longer, you're listing points instead of
  answering.
- Practise the sentence **"I don't know yet, here's how I'd find out"** until it feels normal to
  say. It's the answer that keeps you calm.

## The last line of defence
If a question completely throws you, you can always say:
> *"That connects to the core issue: founders can't tell what they know from what they're
> assuming, and AI makes that worse. On your specific point, I haven't fully worked it out, but
> here's how I'd test it."*

That answer is honest, it brings the discussion back to the core problem, and it ends on a next
step.
