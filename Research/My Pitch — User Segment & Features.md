# Slide 3 — User Segment · ~45 sec

> Who are we building for? **Students who intend to build.**
>
> Our users are **students who intend to build**. We don't define them by degree; we define
> them by intent. That covers an engineering student with a startup idea, an MBA student on a
> venture track, and E-Cell members.
>
> **Primary users are student startup aspirants.** For them, picking the wrong problem doesn't
> cost a grade, it costs the startup. They already ask three questions: *is my problem real,
> has someone taken it, and what makes it mine?*
>
> **Secondary users are mentors, advisors and faculty.** Right now they see the pitch, but not
> the reasoning behind it.
>
> So we're helping students think better, and helping mentors see that thinking.

# Slide 6 — Features · ~75 sec

**Lead-in (coming from the market slides):**
> We've seen who they are and how big the gap is. Here's how we help, and the idea behind all of
> it is: **help with thinking, not answers.**

> Our app works in four steps.
>
> **One: Capture.** Students bring in everything from their research: notes, photos, voice
> recordings, files, straight from the share sheet. For each piece, the app asks one simple
> question: **did you see it, or did you guess it?** That separates what users actually said
> from what the team assumed.
>
> **Two: AI Nudge.** When a student writes a conclusion, the AI **asks about what they added**.
> For example, students say *"I can never find a seat in the library during exams."* The team
> writes *"so the college needs more study spaces."* The AI asks: *"They said it's full during
> exams. Did anyone say it's full the rest of the year?"* It never gives the answer. It just points
> out the step they skipped.
>
> **Three: Log book.** Every decision gets saved **with the evidence behind it**, and the options
> they rejected. So when a prototype fails, the team can go back and see exactly which assumption broke.
>
> **Four: Project portfolio.** That record turns into **proof, ready to pitch**. When a mentor or
> investor asks *"how do you know this is a real problem?"*, the student can show **who actually
> faces the problem** and what they tested.
>
> Underneath that, the app covers the basics: everything in one searchable place, interviews
> turned into notes, teammates working on the same project, and it works **offline**, with
> evidence kept **private on the device**.
>
> Tools like Miro, Notion and Dovetail help you **store** research. **We help you judge it.**

**Close:** *"Help with thinking. Not answers."*

## Questions on my slides

**"Where's the buyer? Who pays?"** *(the buyer column was removed from slide 3)*
> The users are students and mentors. The likely buyers are college incubators and E-Cells, since
> they want better-validated founders coming through. We haven't tested willingness to pay yet.

**"Why are mentors only secondary?"**
> Students do the daily work in the app. Mentors review it. They get the reasoning behind a pitch
> instead of just the slides.

**"Why not every student?"**
> Students with no plan to build have no stake in whether the problem is right. For someone
> starting a company, picking the wrong problem is what kills it.

**"How is AI Nudge different from ChatGPT?"**
> ChatGPT gives an answer, and it gives every team a similar one. AI Nudge only asks questions,
> based on the student's own evidence. The thinking stays with the student.

**"Won't constant nudges get annoying?"**
> It only nudges when a student writes a conclusion or makes a decision, not while they're
> collecting research. And it steps back as they get better at asking the questions themselves.

**"Isn't Log book just a notes app?"**
> Notes store what you wrote. Log book links each decision to the evidence behind it and what you
> rejected, so you can trace back when something fails.

**"So AI writes your pitch deck? Doesn't that contradict 'not answers'?"**
> No. It fills the deck from what the team recorded: their evidence, their decisions, what they
> rejected. It doesn't invent claims. If there's no evidence behind a slide, that slide stays
> empty, which shows the team what they still need to find out.

**"Does it make the pitch deck for me?"** / **"Does Project portfolio write the pitch with AI?"**
> Yes, the pitch deck builds itself automatically as the research and problem develop. Every
> time the team captures evidence or logs a decision, the deck updates: the problem, who said
> it, what was tested, and what was rejected. But it's built from what the team actually
> recorded. The AI doesn't invent the argument, because then we'd be back to generic answers.
> *(Not in the spoken pitch. Use only if asked.)*

**"What if students just tick 'saw it' for everything?"**
> The log book and the mentor view expose it: a claim marked "saw it" with no source attached
> stands out. The app can't force honesty, but it makes weak evidence visible.

**"Why iPad?"**
> Interviews happen in the field, so it works offline, records speech, and keeps evidence private
> on the device. A team can also review the reasoning together on one screen.

**"What would you build first?"**
> Capture and AI Nudge. That's the core of the idea, and we can test it with a few student teams
> before building the log book and portfolio.

**"How is this different from Sprintbase?"**
> Sprintbase guides you through Design Thinking workshops. It doesn't check whether your
> conclusions are backed by evidence. That's our focus.

## Auto-updating pitch deck — added 15 Sep 2026 #key
Part of **Project portfolio**. The deck builds itself as research progresses.

| Deck slide | Filled from |
|---|---|
| Problem | The committed problem in the Log book |
| Evidence | Captures marked **saw it**, with who said it |
| What we tested | Log book decisions and their results |
| What's still open | Assumptions marked **guessed it** and not yet tested |
| Why not the alternatives | Options the team rejected |

**Rule:** it assembles, it doesn't invent. A slide with no evidence behind it stays empty.
That keeps it consistent with *"help with thinking, not answers"* — see [[The Automation Paradox]].
